The full case

Recommendation. Test Capture as a layer inside a till or payments provider, not as a standalone subscription. Spend 12 weeks retiring four risks before building anything (value, usability, feasibility and viability), and stop at the first gate that fails.

Independent restaurants know what they sold, and sometimes what was booked. They don't see what happened before: how many people found them, looked, and went somewhere else. When revenue drops, they can't tell whether the market shrank, they became harder to find, or they stopped turning interest into tables. This case turns that into a product concept, a business case and a plan to test it.

1What I found on the ground

−17%Google profile impressions in twelve months. Google keeps about 18 months of data [1].

Revenue at the restaurant was falling and nobody could say why. I combined till tickets, reservation data and the Google Business Profile.

“We know we're down, and we know how everything is going. But in a traditional business, cash flow is what matters most, and because it never stops, we often don't really understand it. Work like this is how we start to measure it and see its real size.”

Management of the restaurant in the fieldwork, anonymised
  • It was volume. Against the same months in previous years, there were 20% fewer tables, while spend per ticket rose 7% with menu prices unchanged. Without guest counts (next point), I can't tell whether each diner spent more or tables were simply larger.
  • A metric had silently disappeared. Guest counts stopped being recorded after a process change, and nobody noticed.
  • The fix couldn't cost floor time. Management rejected anything that asked staff to log more, such as guest counts or no-shows. Only till tickets were reliable.
  • The leak started upstream. Google profile impressions fell 17% over twelve months: fewer people were seeing the restaurant in local search, before any diner reached the door. Google keeps about 18 months of profile data, so this comparison cannot go back three years like the till data [1].

The pattern is not unique to one venue. In the first half of 2025, 74% of restaurant visits in Spain were booked ahead, two thirds of those bookings were made online, and Google handled 37% of digital bookings, up 27% in a year, according to CoverManager data on 14 million reservations [2]. In a TheFork survey, 24% of owners who had closed a restaurant blamed too few diners [3]. Reservation tools start counting at the booking. Everything before it is invisible to the owner.

Breaking the drop down

Revenue is tables times spend per ticket. Tables depend on how many people search, how many of them see the restaurant, and how many of those come in. Each branch is either measured or named as a gap.

  • Revenuefalling
    • Tables−20%
      • Marketsearches in the areanot measured yetthe check still missing
      • Visibilityprofile impressions−17%the leak the fieldwork found
      • Conversiontables per profile actionnot measured yet
    • Spend per ticket+7%
      • Price per itemunchanged
      • Diners per ticketunknown, guest counts lost

Capture measures the two gaps on the tables branch every week. The third, diners per ticket, stays open by design: counting guests would add a task to the floor.

2Whose problem this solves, and why now

+27%growth in a year of Google's share of digital restaurant bookings in Spain [2].

Three people have a stake, each with a different need:

  • The owner needs to understand a cash flow that never stops, and why it moved.
  • The manager needs one clear action, never more admin.
  • The till or payments provider needs merchants who stay.

Owners also differ in how much the problem hurts, and so in what they would pay. Three segments, with willingness to pay as a hypothesis the interviews price separately:

SegmentWhat they needLikely willingness to pay
Single site, tables fallingTo know why, this weekHighest: the pain is current. The first segment.
Single site, stable tradeAn early warningLow: nothing feels broken
Small group, 2 to 5 sitesTo spot the weak siteHigh per group, if sites can be compared

Why now

  • Google is becoming a booking channel. Its share of digital restaurant bookings in Spain grew 27% in a year [2].
  • Payments companies want the diner's first step. American Express agreed to buy TheFork in 2026 [4].
  • Analytics is now free inside tills. Spanish tills bundle it from 50 € a month [11], so the value has to be the diagnosis, not the dashboard.
  • AI makes one-venue reports affordable. A plain-language weekly report no longer needs an analyst per restaurant.

3The concept: a weekly capture report

A one-page report for the owner every Monday, built only from data the restaurant already produces: its Google Business Profile and its till, plus reservations where the venue keeps them reliably. Google's figures arrive up to five days late, so each report covers the last week with complete data [1][5]. Working name: Capture.

  • It follows one outcome: weekly tables, counted from till tickets.
  • It explains that outcome with three signals. Market: searches for the category in the region, from Google Trends, read monthly because low-volume searches show up as zero [6]. Visibility: profile impressions on Search and Maps. Conversion: a search-to-table index, tickets against profile actions, read as a four-week trend. It is an index, not a funnel rate, because walk-ins (26% of visits in the CoverManager data [2]) and regulars never touch Google.
  • It names the cause of a bad week. Fewer searches in the area is a market problem. The same searches but fewer impressions is a visibility problem. The same impressions but fewer tables is a conversion problem.
  • It recommends one action for the week, chosen from a fixed set mapped to each cause. An LLM only writes it in plain language, so it cannot invent advice the numbers don't support.
  • It asks nothing of the floor team. That is a design constraint, not a feature.

For owners of independent restaurants who see revenue move but not why, Capture shows whether the market, their visibility or their conversion moved, and where diners are lost between Google and the table, using data they already have and no extra work for their team. Reservation tools start counting at the booking. Capture starts at the search.

How the report earns action

A diagnosis nobody acts on has no value, and my first proposal to an owner was a no. So the report is designed around how owners decide:

  • Value first. Every owner starts with a free leak check on their last 12 months. It gives something before asking for anything, and it is how Capture gets access to the data.
  • The owner picks the action. The report suggests one, the owner confirms it, and the next Monday opens with what happened. The loop closes on their own decision.
  • Neighbours as reference. Where data allows, it compares visibility with similar restaurants nearby, the comparison owners already make in their heads.
  • Losses in tables and euros. Owners think in cash flow, as the quote above shows. "12 tables below your usual week, about 500 € in sales" moves an owner; "index down 4%" does not.
  • Their own numbers only. Every figure comes from Google or their till. No invented urgency, no countdowns.
  • Through people they trust. A quarter of Barcelona's establishments belong to a trade or neighbourhood association, and a third of restaurants do [7]. Introductions through them reach owners where trust already exists.

4The service, end to end

Most tools are designed for the bad week. This one is designed around the normal week. The owner's need, in their own words, is to understand a cash flow that never stops, and a report that only speaks when something breaks teaches people to ignore it. So most Mondays Capture simply makes the week legible in tables and euros. It diagnoses only when a week falls outside the usual range.

The blueprint also separates two people the fieldwork showed are different: the owner decides and the manager acts. Floor staff never appear, by design.

5The business case

Market

≈ 13,000independent restaurants in Catalonia, of 16,301 in total [8].

Catalonia has 16,301 restaurants, plus 23,781 bars [8]. Chains hold close to 31% of restaurant sales in Spain, but with about 16,300 branded units against 83,714 restaurant premises they are at most one venue in five [9]. In Barcelona, only 5.1% of establishments are franchises [7].

Taking 80% as independent gives a serviceable market of about 13,000 restaurants in Catalonia, 9,000 of them in the province of Barcelona. The figure is conservative: almost half of the establishments surveyed in Barcelona are bar-restaurants [7], many with table service but counted as bars. I found no public figure for how many keep an active Google profile, so that is a filter to check in the interviews.

Price, designed before the product

Price is set in discovery, not after the build. In the ten interviews, after the problem questions and never before, each owner says at what monthly price Capture would seem so cheap they'd doubt it, a good deal, expensive, and too expensive. The answers give a price range per segment. 59 € is the starting hypothesis, not the decision. Owners also rank features: the ones that drive willingness to pay go into the paid package, and the ones nobody would pay for are dropped, not bundled.

PackageWhat's in itPrice to test
Leak checkOne-off report on the last 12 months: the three signals and the biggest leakFree
WeeklyMonday report, the cause, one action and the result of last week's action59 € a month per site
GroupWeekly for every site, plus a comparison across sitesPer site, with a volume discount

One rule runs across every package: nothing that needs staff input. It is the feature most likely to turn a yes into a no.

Who else is close

Malou manages local search and reviews for more than 2,000 restaurants. Toast connects the Google profile, but for reviews only. Spanish tills such as Qamarero and Last.app report sales in detail, without profile metrics next to them [10]. I found no one publicly joining the Google funnel with till data. That is either a gap or a sign that it is hard, and the first partner conversation should find out which.

The stronger model: embedded in a partner

50,000+restaurants in TheFork, which American Express agreed to buy in 2026 [4].

Whoever already holds the transaction data (a payments, point-of-sale or reservation provider) can offer Capture to its merchants. It also solves the hardest technical problem: one integration instead of dozens of tills. For the partner, the value is merchant retention, not subscription revenue.

The direction of the market supports it. In June 2026, American Express agreed to buy TheFork, a network of more than 50,000 restaurants, for about 700 million dollars [4]: a payments company buying its way into how diners find restaurants. There are three ways to structure a deal.

ModelWho paysWhen it makes sense
Free layerThe partner, for retentionCost to serve is very low and the report is fully automated
Premium add-onThe merchant, with revenue shareOnly part of the base values it
White-label licenceThe partner, as a fixed feeThe partner wants the product without building it

What has to be true for a free layer: the churn it prevents must pay for serving every merchant. Spanish till plans run from 50 € to 175 € a month per location, with analytics included [11]. With an illustrative partner revenue of 70 € a month per merchant (840 € a year), on a one-year view:

Cost to serve per merchantAnnual churn reduction needed to break even
1 € a month1.4 points
3 € a month4.3 points

The bar is demanding unless serving costs are close to zero. That is why the premium add-on is the likelier first deal, and the free layer a later step.

Standalone, as a floor

7.8%of Spanish companies closed in 2023, a floor for any churn estimate [13].

Reservation software in Spain lists from free tiers up to 299 € a month [12], and tills give analytics away. Sold directly at the 59 € hypothesis, Capture would sit low in that range. At 1%, 3% and 5% of the serviceable market (130, 391 and 652 restaurants), that is about 92,000, 277,000 and 462,000 € a year. Unit economics decide whether it survives. With an assumed 80% gross margin:

I found no public churn benchmark for restaurant software. Closures alone set a floor: in Spain, 7.8% of companies closed in 2023, and only 42% of those founded in 2018 were still active five years later, across all sectors [13].

What the numbers say

Standalone only works with low churn and cheap acquisition, which is rare when selling to independent restaurants one by one. Embedded turns the same product into a retention tool for a company that already has the merchants and the data. Every figure above is a hypothesis to test, and the partner case is the first one I would take to a client.

6Four risks to retire before building

Value: owners act on a weekly diagnosis

Why it could be false
My first proposal to an owner was a clear no. Insight is not adoption.
Test
Concierge pilot: 3 restaurants, 4 weeks, report built by hand.
Stop if
Fewer than 2 of 3 act on at least one recommendation.

Usability: owners understand it in two minutes

Why it could be false
Owners read it on a phone, between services. A dense report won't survive that.
Test
5 owners read a mock report on their phone, with no explanation, at the end of their interview.
Stop if
Fewer than 4 of 5 can say the cause and the action within two minutes.

Feasibility: Google and till data are enough

Why it could be false
Profile data is aggregated, arrives up to five days late and needs API access approved by Google. Walk-ins aren't attributed.
Test
Each pilot venue's last 12 months of profile data against its ticket counts, pulled in week 3.
Stop if
No stable relationship in at least 2 venues.

Viability: someone pays for it

Why it could be false
Tills already include analytics at no extra cost, so owners may see no reason to pay again [11].
Test
Price questions in the 10 owner interviews, plus one conversation with a till or payments provider.
Stop if
Fewer than 3 of 10 owners place 59 € inside their acceptable range, and no partner sees retention or upsell value above its cost to serve.

7Roadmap: 12 weeks to a decision

  1. Weeks 1 to 2Discover

    10 owner interviews in Barcelona: the journey, the leaks, whether they keep an active Google profile, a usability test of the mock report and the price range. One conversation with a till or payments provider to test the partner case. Output: validated journey, price range per segment, usability result and partner interest

  2. Weeks 3 to 6Concierge pilot

    Start with a backfill: each venue's last 12 months of profile data against its tickets. Then 3 restaurants receive a hand-built weekly report from their own exports, with the action chosen from the fixed set. Metric: recommendations acted on

  3. Weeks 7 to 12Build the MVP

    Automate the Google profile and till data pipeline, keep the report format that worked, and track weekly use. Metric: change in weekly tables, explained by the three signals

  4. Week 12Decide

    Go embedded with a partner, go standalone, or stop, based on the four stop criteria above.

  5. After week 12If the gates hold

    A pilot with one partner in months 4 to 6, launch to its merchant base in months 7 to 12, then a second partner. Metric: share of merchants with weekly tables back in range

How the interviews run

  • Start with the last time revenue dropped: what they noticed, what they opened, what they did. Past behaviour, not opinions about the future.
  • Show nothing until the end. Then hand over the mock report and watch them read it.
  • Ask about price last, so the pitch doesn't anchor the answer.
  • Two people per interview: one asks, one takes notes. Notes are shared the same day.

How each gate is decided

Each gate is a 30-minute decision meeting: a one-page pre-read sent the day before, one person who makes the call, and stop criteria agreed before the evidence comes in.

North-star metric: weekly tables. Diagnostic signals: area searches, profile impressions and the search-to-table index. Guardrail: zero added tasks for floor staff.

8What this case can and can't claim

  • One restaurant. The diagnosis comes from a single anonymised venue. It shows the problem exists, not how common it is.
  • Two time windows. Till data covers six months against the same months in three years. Google data covers twelve months, the most it allows.
  • No guest counts. Spend per diner is unknown, so the 7% rise is per ticket.
  • Visibility or market. The fieldwork points to visibility. Area search data is the check that would rule out a shrinking market.
  • Public sources only. Market figures come from published data. Paid sector reports were not consulted.
  • Estimates. The 80% independent share, the partner revenue, the cost to serve and the churn levels are assumptions, stated as such and tested in the roadmap.

9How I built this

This case is also a test of how I work with AI. It sped up most of the process; auditing what it produced did not get faster. Many of the structures were its proposals, so deciding meant choosing among them and rejecting some.

Where this method can fail

  • Without the fieldwork, this would be desk research. The three insights that shape the case (the lost guest counts, the veto on floor tasks and the owner's words on cash flow) are in no database.
  • Synthetic reviewers share one model's blind spots. Four simulated roles are closer to one opinion than to four. The next step is the same review with a real owner, a product designer and an engineer, before building anything.
  • The research only reached public, indexed sources. Paid reports were out of reach, so the market figures rest on what is published.

How I work

I turn messy operating data into decisions people can act on: the problem, the evidence, the case and the gates that decide it. In a product team, designers own the experience and engineers the pipeline, and we decide together at each gate. Working directly with a business, I bring the same structure and keep the stop criteria agreed before the evidence comes in.
