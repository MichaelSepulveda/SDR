A few questions worth answering

Why so many sectors, and so many moves?

I’ve moved across sectors and countries for the same reason: to widen the way I understand problems, people and what’s possible. Each move added something different, from analytical foundations in Colombia to international perspective, exploration, operating experience and, now, a deliberate decision to stay and build in Spain.

Read the longer story

ColombiaThe foundation

Colombia is where I built the foundation. I studied economics because I wanted to understand the structures behind the problems I was seeing: how institutions, markets and incentives shape what happens to businesses, communities and people.

It was also where I first saw how much the people around you can expand your sense of what is possible. In one of my first roles, I worked with a young director bringing an innovation mindset into a deeply traditional industry. Seeing someone challenge the assumptions of an established sector from within stayed with me. He was also one of the people who encouraged me to take the opportunity to go to Washington.

That curiosity took me into industry research, public policy and entrepreneurship. It gave me the analytical lens I still rely on today, but also an early sense that understanding a system is only useful if it helps you change something within it.

Washington D.C.The perspective shift

D.C. didn’t introduce me to ambition or talented people. It changed the scale and diversity of the environment around me.

It was my first real international professional experience, and suddenly I was working alongside people from very different countries, backgrounds and stages of their careers. What struck me was not simply how accomplished they were, but how differently they could approach problems that were often surprisingly similar.

It widened my frame of reference. I came back to Colombia wanting to keep putting myself in environments where the people around me could challenge my assumptions, expose me to different ways of thinking and change the size of the opportunities I could imagine.

Europe & AsiaThe exploration

After the pandemic, I wanted to see what life and work could look like outside the path I already knew. I planned roughly a year of travel before spending some time in Australia, starting with three months in Southern Europe and then continuing to Southeast Asia.

What I didn’t expect was to spend another seven months there, living as a digital nomad. The journey became more important than I had planned. It exposed me to different cultures, people and ways of working, but it also changed my relationship with time. I realised that while some things benefit from moving fast, others only become meaningful when you give them enough time. Travel was one of them.

AustraliaThe test

Australia was meant to be a relatively short chapter. By then, though, I was less interested in rushing to the next destination simply because that had been the plan.

I went there to study and experience another way of living and working. I stayed for two years. In that time, I found myself starting again professionally, learning an operation from the ground up, moving into management and eventually helping integrate a restaurant into a multi-brand group.

It was a very different kind of education. I went from analysing organisations from a desk to seeing what decisions look like when a team has to make them work on a busy Friday night. It taught me that I could enter an unfamiliar environment, learn quickly and build my way into responsibility.

SpainThe decision to stay

Spain wasn't originally on the map. What began as an unexpected move became a conscious decision to build here. I completed my master's in entrepreneurship and innovation, started working with local businesses, and invested in learning Catalan and building a network in Catalonia.

For the first time in this journey, the question is less about where to go next and more about what I can build if I stay long enough. That's the decision: to keep the international exposure that shaped me, while giving the work, relationships and opportunities here enough time to compound.

The thread underneath all of these moves has been surprisingly consistent. I like unfamiliar environments, people who challenge my assumptions and problems I don’t fully understand yet.

What I want now is different from what I wanted when I first left Colombia: to keep that exposure, but stay somewhere long enough to see the results compound.

Ever had a good idea die in the first meeting?

My first proposal to my boss was a clear no. The idea made sense, but the solution asked too much from a team already stretched thin. So I didn't try to sell it harder. I parked it, broke the problem down and looked for what we could test with the data and resources we already had. That smaller experiment opened the door to a broader engagement. It shaped how I work now: treat the first solution as a hypothesis, learn from the constraint and keep moving until you find something people can actually adopt.

Why hospitality and wine?

Because I'm drawn to industries that are essential, established and often overlooked when people talk about innovation. Hospitality taught me where operations stop being theory; wine showed me how much value can still be unlocked when traditional businesses rethink the way they use data, processes and technology. In Brisbane, I helped integrate a trattoria into a multi-brand group. In Tarragona, I worked with two family-owned wine businesses through an acquisition and built workflows that cut one critical process from five days to under two hours. I like the challenge of bringing modern tools and ways of working into sectors that were already functioning long before anyone called it transformation.

Some of the work I'm proudest of paid me nothing.

I co-founded TrasPAZar, a pro bono consulting initiative for entrepreneurs from conflict-affected communities in Colombia. We secured four client projects, coordinated 15 volunteers across four countries and ran workshops on entrepreneurship and strategy, each with measurable objectives.

I still believe the best strategy is the one that leaves people better off.

Good ideas don't move on their own.

My master's thesis used network analysis to map how knowledge moves between research institutions and companies across European innovation ecosystems.

It changed the way I think about innovation. Good ideas and technical capability matter, but so do the connections around them: who talks to whom, where information gets stuck and whether the right people can act on it.
